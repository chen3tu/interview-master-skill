Keep your preparation in English while treating Japan as the hiring market. The employer’s actual terms, interview language, and role expectations should determine the adjustments; location alone does not tell us the company’s practices.

Questions you can ask about compensation:

1. “Could you break down the package into fixed base pay, guaranteed payments, variable bonuses, equity, and allowances, and confirm the currency and whether the figures are gross?”
2. “How is fixed pay distributed through the year? Are any additional payments guaranteed, and are they already included in the quoted annual amount?”
3. “How is the variable bonus determined, and what affects eligibility, payment timing, and first-year proration? What amount is a target rather than a guarantee?”
4. “Does the quoted package include any fixed overtime component? If so, could you explain its amount, the hours it corresponds to, and how additional work is handled under the proposed terms?”
5. “Which allowances or benefits would apply to this role—for example, commuting, relocation, or housing—and are they recurring payments, reimbursements, or one-time support?”
6. “Are there any differences in compensation during a probationary period, and when are salary reviews normally considered?”
7. “If equity is included, could you explain the grant type, vesting, exercise or purchase requirements, and what happens when employment ends?”

These are questions to establish the offer’s structure, not claims that every Japanese employer uses those components. Ask for the written breakdown so that you can compare guaranteed annual cash, variable target pay, one-time payments, and equity separately without double-counting bonus payments.

To localize your interview preparation, first confirm the language of each interview and the language used in daily work. If Japanese proficiency is required, ask what tasks require it and describe your current ability accurately; an English conversation with a recruiter does not settle that requirement.

Use the actual job description to map your examples to decisions, responsibilities, and collaboration expectations. Ask about the team’s decision-making process, approval responsibilities, and coordination across locations rather than assuming a particular communication style. Keep your original job titles alongside any explanation needed for unfamiliar titles; do not turn a translation into a higher level of seniority.

Confirm the expected résumé format, interview stages, work-sample requirements, and scheduling time zone. Prepare a concise introduction, a project example with clear individual contribution, and a reason for wanting this specific role and location. Those answers should come from your experience and interests, not generalizations about Japanese culture.

The remaining facts that would materially change the preparation are the employer, role, working language, and written compensation breakdown. The next useful step is to obtain the interview format and package components from the recruiter; no current legal or tax conclusion is needed to prepare these questions.
