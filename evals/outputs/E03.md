Here is a worked example first. All numbers below are invented for teaching; they are not Lee's project results.

Suppose 2,000 eligible new accounts are randomly assigned before exposure: 1,000 receive a new onboarding checklist and 1,000 keep the current flow. Activation means completing a defined first-value event within seven days. The checklist group has 180 activations and the control group has 150.

The observed activation rates are 18% and 15%. The difference is **3 percentage points**, or **20% relative to the control rate**: (18% − 15%) / 15%. Neither is “20 percentage points.” This is an estimated treatment effect under a valid design, not certainty that every extra activation was caused by the checklist.

Before trusting it, I would verify that assignment was random and preserved, eligibility and tracking were identical, there was no material contamination across groups, and accounts had equal observation time. I would analyze assigned eligible accounts rather than only checklist completers, because completion itself can select more motivated users. I would estimate uncertainty rather than call a 30-account difference conclusive. Under a simple independent-account approximation, the standard error of the rate difference is about 1.66 percentage points; an approximate 95% interval is about −0.25 to +6.25 percentage points. That is compatible with no activation effect as well as a meaningful positive effect. This approximation is illustrative, not a substitute for the actual design analysis.

Now suppose week-four activity is 90/1,000 in the checklist group and 100/1,000 in control. Those rates are 9% and 10% among all assigned accounts. They do not establish that retention worsened, but they do mean we should not advertise a proven durable improvement. We cannot divide those counts by activations and call the result “retention of activated accounts” without knowing the users overlap and defining that estimand carefully.

The decision is to avoid a broad success claim, inspect implementation and downstream behavior, and collect the planned amount of evidence with guardrails. If serious user harm appears, stop or roll back regardless of whether the planned statistical endpoint has arrived. Do not keep extending the test until a favorable result appears.

The causal question is: **What would these accounts have done without the new checklist?** A credible control estimates that missing counterfactual. A before/after increase alone mixes the intervention with seasonality, acquisition changes, other engineering releases and shifts in user intent.

An alternative, if randomization is infeasible, is a phased rollout with a comparable untreated group and pre-period data. A difference-in-differences analysis can subtract changes also seen in the comparison group, but it depends on assumptions such as comparable trends and no differential concurrent changes. It is weaker when those assumptions are implausible. Choosing a fancy method does not create missing evidence.

There is a second attribution question: the effect of the checklist versus Lee's personal contribution. If a treatment bundles a checklist and platform changes, even a valid experiment estimates the bundle's effect. It does not allocate credit to the checklist coordinator or engineering separately. Separating components requires an appropriate design or additional evidence; personal contribution can instead be stated through actual actions.

For your real project, the truthful answer is:

“I cannot establish the causal impact from the information I currently have. I coordinated the onboarding checklist and weekly issue reviews with support; engineering delivered the platform changes. The 20% in my résumé lacks a recoverable denominator and comparison period, so I cannot substantiate it. I would first reconstruct the metric and then look for a credible comparison, concurrent changes and downstream retention evidence.”

Your next drill, after studying the example: explain how your conclusion changes if the checklist and engineering changes reach treatment accounts together. The key distinction is a possible effect of the package versus an unsupported estimate of your individual impact. No practice response has been observed yet.
